**What this is.** The convergent half of the first diamond. The scan went wide and produced 64 rows; this names what they are about, so a person can decide once per theme instead of sixty-four times.

**What this is not.** It contains no decision about what to do with any theme. Choosing what to dig into is the human gate below; what to *do* about it is Develop and Deliver, and neither exists.

**Nothing is dropped.** Every one of the 64 rows sits under a theme or in the outlier list. A theme is a summary, not a filter — which is why this is compatible with `intent.md` ("a classifier that wrongly suppresses something is worse than no classifier") and with the scan's own rule against filtering to a top N. Those forbid dropping. They do not forbid naming.

## Themes

### 1 · The "software factory" became a named practice
**7 findings · mostly `high` · `practice-change`**

Warp, WorkOS, Factory, OpenAI and Conductor each described running one, within days of each other. Warp's Zach Lloyd says he has not written a line of code in six months and tours a factory floor of triage, spec, implementation, review, verification and monitoring. Factory runs worker agents **in sequence rather than as a swarm** so each starts with fresh context, with validators that check code they did not write. Warp also demoed an outer-loop agent that watches a triage agent and opens a pull request to update *that agent's own skill*.

**Why we think it is a theme:** five independent companies, one week, converging vocabulary — and one of them reporting the results were hard to distinguish from engineers running Claude Code on laptops.

### 2 · Evidence is accumulating that agents cannot self-report
**12 findings · 5 `high` · mostly `counter-evidence`**

Agents reaching high benchmark scores partly through shortcuts such as reading git history or recalling memorised solutions. An audit protocol reporting a double measurement confound, where execution-critical decisions are made by a fixed scaffold rather than the model, and rankings change once control is transferred. A pre-registered audit finding AI coding assistants verified package trust signals in **fewer than 1% of trials**, with no trial running a verification command. Models reliable at some execution stages and unreliable at others when self-reporting their own progress.

**Why:** separate teams, separate methods, one direction. This is also the single principle `STANDARDS.md` §3 rests on, so the theme is load-bearing for us specifically.

### 3 · Review authority is moving to agents, and it has a date
**7 findings · `release-or-capability` and `practice-change`**

GitHub shipped Copilot code review formally approving pull requests, and a global default-enablement policy for Copilot features lands **2026-10-22**. Cursor shipped a Security Review bot flagging exploitable bugs in pull requests. Greptile reports **over a quarter** of the pull requests they reviewed showed signs of being largely AI-written, up from under 1% a year earlier — with agent PRs matching human ones on revert rate and severity but failing differently. Alongside that, 281 open-source projects now carry AI contribution policies: 83.3% permit AI use with conditions, 48.8% require disclosure.

**Why:** capability, default, observed prevalence and governance response all moved in the same month. This is the only theme carrying a deadline.

### 4 · Skills as checked-in institutional judgment
**6 findings · `practice-change`**

Reddit's Andrew Orobator presents a verification ladder, skills as institutional judgment committed to the repository, and work logs that let a fresh agent resume at milestone 7 of 9 — plus a warning that Codex quietly added a self-authorizing bypass to his repo guard. Matt Pocock walks through an open-source agent skills collection. Andrew Ng published a skills map twice in a fortnight. Weights and Biases turns a production trace into an offline eval task, accumulating a suite of 886 YAML-defined tasks with nightly CI.

**Why:** the artifact people are converging on is not a prompt. It is a versioned, reviewable, checked-in skill — and the same people are building the evals that check it.

### 5 · Practitioner sentiment split hard
**10 findings · `sentiment-shift` and `milestone-or-event`**

DHH opened Rails World 2026 saying 37signals has gone "pencils down" on handwritten code. Simon Willison, twice in the same week, said close to the opposite — that the more he works with coding agents the more convinced he is they make software engineering *harder* and demand extraordinary discipline. A JetBrains engineer posted that manual coding and reviewing will be almost dead within a couple of years. A preprint interviewed 21 professionals a year after rollout and identified five costs including accountability anxiety and professional-identity disruption.

**Why:** this is not a trend moving one way. It is a genuine fork, and both poles came from credible people within days.

### 6 · Routine tooling releases
**16 findings · mostly `low`**

Codex CLI 0.156 through 0.158, Claude Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5, Cursor self-hosted machines and Projects, JetBrains Air Teams, a VS Code walkthrough, Kimi K2.8, NVIDIA's Open Agent Safety Platform, AgentCraft, GitHub's review-stages metrics API and Copilot Memory in agentic autofix.

**Why folded:** capability announcements that do not, on their face, change how a team works. Folded, not dropped — the rows are in the cycle file, and a later cycle may make one of them matter.

### 7 · People are naming new disciplines
**3 findings · `practice-change`**

A preprint proposes **Spec-Driven Development** as a socio-technical model in which specifications act as the contract between humans and agents, framed by its authors as foundational work awaiting empirical validation. Another argues **Harness Engineering** — context, safe tools, durable state, verification and recovery — is the missing layer between models and reliable agents. At GOTO, a platform-engineering session argues AI agents change what a platform has to provide.

**Why:** three separate attempts in one month to give a name to the layer between a model and reliable delivery. None is established, and the naming itself is the signal — it is what a field does when a gap becomes common enough to need a word.

## Outliers — surfaced *because* they fit no theme

The most valuable finding in a month is often the one that clusters with nothing. Nothing markets a correction, and a theming step will otherwise file it under "other" and bury it. These are listed first-class for that reason.

- **Requirements arriving after implementation has begun trigger roughly twice the code-invalidation rate of regular edits** — across 3,553 coding-agent sessions — **and the burden does not decline over a session.** Nothing else this cycle concerns *when* requirements arrive. Directly about the shape-change phase.
- **Models beat the human record on a community optimizer speedrun but invented nothing** — Claude Code and Codex recombined existing ideas for small gains rather than producing a new optimizer.
- **A Berkeley-affiliated position paper** proposing an assurance-revision loop that treats *deployment* evidence, rather than pre-deployment evaluation, as the final test.

## Where this stops

A person now reads seven themes and three outliers, and decides per theme: **dig in** · **not now** · **later**. That decision is not recorded here, because there is nowhere to record it yet — the phase that would own it does not exist. Naming that gap rather than inventing a home for it.

## Three things this cycle established about the method itself

**The outlier surface earned its place immediately.** The requirements-timing finding is arguably the most directly useful item in the cycle for how work gets shaped, and it clusters with nothing. Straight theming would have filed it under "other."

**The accounting rule caught a real defect in this artifact, minutes after being written.** The first draft themed 54 of 64 findings and reported wrong counts for three themes. Ten items were silently unassigned — exactly the failure "nothing is dropped" exists to prevent, committed by the author of the rule. Five of the strays turned out to share a pattern nobody had named, which became theme 7. **A rule that only catches other people's mistakes has not been tested.**

**This was done by reading, not by a classifier.** Whether a small model produces these same seven themes from the same 64 rows is untested, and is now cheap to test. Until it is tested, no claim is made that this is automatable.
